The thing it measures is not one thing.

Language models are tested on causal reasoning and given a percentage. We took that percentage apart. On the first two rungs of the standard test, most of what the score counts as a reasoning failure is not a reasoning failure at all.

Background

How the test works

CLadder is a set of 10,112 causal questions. Each one describes a small imaginary world, gives you some probabilities, and asks a yes or no question about cause and effect.

The questions come in three levels, which the field calls rungs.

The gap between rung 1 and rung 2 is the whole point of causal inference. Smokers have yellow fingers, but scrubbing your fingers will not protect you from cancer. Seeing and doing are different questions, and a model that cannot tell them apart is not reasoning about causes.

Every question has two parts

To answer one of these, a model has to do two separate things. First, work out which quantity answers the question. Second, compute that quantity from the numbers in the prompt.

Only the first part is causal reasoning. The second part is arithmetic. The benchmark scores the final yes or no, so a wrong answer could come from either part, and the score cannot tell you which.

Our whole approach is to check the first part on its own.

The problem

Why grading is hard

Imagine marking a maths test where the answer key says 1/2. A student writes 0.5. Another writes 2/4. A grader that compares text marks both wrong.

Causal expressions have the same problem, but worse, because whether two of them mean the same thing depends on the diagram of what causes what.

The notation, in one paragraph

P(recovery | took drug) means: among people who took the drug, the share who recovered. That is watching.

P(recovery | do(took drug)) means: if we reached in and made everyone take it, the share who would recover. That is doing. The do is the part that makes it causal.

Sometimes those two are equal and sometimes they are not, and which case you are in depends entirely on the arrows in the diagram. So you cannot decide whether two expressions match by looking at the expressions. You have to check them against the diagram.

Method

Building a better grader

There is an existing tool for this called DoVerifier. It works like a word ladder. CAT becomes COT becomes COG, one letter at a time, and every step has to be a real word.

Causal inference has three rewriting rules, known as do-calculus. Each one says: if the diagram looks like this, you are allowed to rewrite that expression as this other one. DoVerifier takes the model's answer, applies every legal rewrite, then every legal rewrite of those, and checks whether it can reach the answer key. If a path exists, the two mean the same thing.

Where it broke
We ran it first and it recovered nothing at all. Not one question, on any rung. The reason turned out to be structural rather than a bug. Do-calculus is a language for talking about one world at a time. Rung 3 questions ask about two worlds at once, the one that happened and the one that did not, and no amount of rewriting can express that. So every rung 3 question fell through to plain text matching, which is exactly the thing the tool exists to replace.

What we built instead

Rather than proving two expressions equal by rewriting, test it by simulation.

  1. Build a few hundred tiny imaginary worlds that all obey the diagram. Every variable gets a randomly chosen rule for how it responds to its causes.
  2. Compute both expressions in every one of those worlds.
  3. If they disagree even once, they are different quantities, and you now have a concrete example of them disagreeing.
  4. If they agree everywhere, they are the same quantity.

It is the difference between proving two formulas equal with algebra and just plugging in a thousand numbers. Less elegant, but it works on the questions do-calculus cannot express, and it hands you the disagreeing case for free.

This method comes from work by Balke and Pearl in 1994 on representing every possible small causal model as a finite set of response patterns. Because CLadder's diagrams have five variables at most, the set is small enough to search.

Four possible verdicts, not two

VerdictMeaning
equivalentIdentical in every world.

sign flippedAlways the exact negative. The two halves of a comparison were written in the wrong order. A slip, not a misunderstanding.

Methodology, honestly

Six corrections we had to make

Every one of these was found by reading failures one at a time, and every one of them raised the score. That pattern should make you suspicious, so here is the full list with the evidence for each. Judge them individually.

CorrectionWhy it is justified
Shorthand for a comparison. The answer key writes P(Y|X) where the question asks whether Y is more likely given X than given not X. The benchmark's own answer key uses this shorthand. A model writing out the comparison in full is writing the same thing.
A typo in the answer key. Every question of one type has a bracket where a parenthesis belongs. A single character, identical across all 156 questions. Repairing it is a no-op on every well formed key.
Scaled quantities count as equal. One answer key differs from a model's answer by a positive multiplier. Both always have the same sign, so both answer the same yes or no question. Verified across 562 simulated worlds with no exceptions.
The treatment comes from the key, not from word order. The order in which conditions are listed varies. Reading the treatment off position built comparisons in the wrong variable.
One question type is not an expression task at all. Its answer key is a placeholder sentence. It asks whether a set of variables is valid to adjust for. That is a different check. We removed it rather than score it wrong.
The answer key for indirect effects does not match the textbook definition. Confirmed not equivalent by simulation. Models that write the standard definition are marked wrong. We flag this rather than patch it.
Why this list is in the open
Reported as a single final number, these would look like tuning. Reported as a list with reasons, each one can be checked and rejected on its own. Four of the six are corrections to the answer key rather than to our code.

Finding 1

A lot of right answers were being marked wrong

We ran the verifier over 4,671 reasoning traces from Gemini 2.5 Flash, covering rungs 1 and 2. Comparing text marks an answer right only when it matches the key character for character. Comparing meaning does much better.

Across both rungs, 840 of 4,658 questions were being marked wrong for how they were written rather than what they said. Rung 1 goes from 89.6% to 99.1%. Rung 2 goes from 58.8% to 93.8%.

This is worth sitting with. It means that before you can ask whether a model reasons about causes, you have to fix the grader, because the grader was wrong about one question in six.

Finding 2

The causal step usually works, even when the answer is wrong

Now the real question. When a model gets one of these wrong, did it fail to work out which quantity to compute, or did it work that out correctly and then fumble the rest?

We can answer that, because the verifier checks the first part on its own.

On average effect questions, Gemini wrote down exactly the right quantity in 88% of the cases it got wrong.

So the errors are not happening at the causal step. The model knows it needs to compare a world where everyone takes the drug against a world where nobody does. It writes precisely that. Then something later goes wrong.

One caution. The direction of the gap is not stable across models, so we would not lean on the claim that wrong answers do better. The claim that survives both models is the level: around nine in ten errors had the causal step right.

Validity

Checking ourselves

There is an obvious objection, and it is the right one to raise.

Reasoning traces are prose. To check which quantity a model was computing, we use a second model, Claude Sonnet, to translate that prose into notation. It sees the trace and also the original question. So maybe it was guessing the right notation from the question, and never reading the reasoning at all.

That worry is not idle, because a question reading translator would produce exactly the pattern we found. The question is the same whether the model got the arithmetic right or wrong, so the rate would look flat either way.

Two tests

Hide the question. Give the translator only the reasoning trace.

Swap the reasoning. Give the translator a trace belonging to a completely different question of a different type, keeping the real question. If the score holds up, the trace was never being read.

The second test needed a fix partway through. Our first version swapped traces within the same question type, which does nothing, because every question of a given type has the identical answer key. Five types, five keys, 4,658 questions. Swapping inside a type swaps a trace for one aiming at the same answer.

With the question hidden, the pattern holds: 87.5% of correct answers and 90.6% of wrong ones had the right quantity. That is the least confounded version of the finding, and it is the one to quote.

Not every question type survives this

Splitting the two effects apart per question type shows that some categories are carried by their phrasing.

Finding 3

So where do the errors actually happen?

Knowing that the causal step succeeded is a negative result. To make it positive we need to say what failed instead.

The answer key lays the whole chain out: which quantity, which numbers to pull from the prompt, how to combine them, and which way the final comparison points. So for a wrong answer whose causal step was right, there are only four places left for it to have gone wrong, and the numbers written in the trace tell you which.

A worked example. On one question the model correctly identified that it needed an instrumental variable, correctly read every probability from the prompt, and then noticed that the instrument does not actually vary. It stated that clearly and then produced an answer anyway. The causal reasoning is visible and correct. The step after it is where the failure lives.

One bucket we do not trust yet
The 21% labelled "right value, wrong yes or no" is mostly something else, and the next section explains what. We also could not parse the answer key's arithmetic on 13 questions, and one case suggests our parser sometimes reads the wrong line. The 59% figure is the most robust of these, because it requires the correct value to be absent from the trace, which no tolerance setting can fake.

Finding 4

Some of the questions are coin flips

Chasing down that suspicious bucket turned up something about the benchmark itself.

Two examples. A question asks whether an outcome is more likely than not. The true probability is exactly 0.500. The model computes 0.5034 and says yes. Another model computes 0.4972 and says no. Both are correct to within rounding. One is marked right and one is marked wrong, and which is which is decided by the third decimal place.

This is not a model failure and it is not a grader failure. It is a property of the questions. A model that does everything right and rounds at the fourth decimal will get some of these wrong, and no amount of better reasoning will help.

It also means a small part of every reported CLadder score is noise, and it is not evenly spread. Correlation questions carry four times more of it than average effect questions.

Finding 5

You cannot study reasoning you cannot see

Everything above depends on reading what a model actually did. That turns out to vary enormously by provider, in a way that shapes what any study of this kind can conclude.

This has a direct consequence. The claim "Gemini identified the right quantity" is really "the summary of Gemini's reasoning names the right quantity". The control tests show the summaries do carry real information, so the finding stands, but the wording matters.

It also decided which model we could use for the error breakdown in the previous section. On Gemini, only 10 of the average effect errors contain any arithmetic to inspect. On Sonnet the working is all there, which is why that analysis uses Sonnet.

Aside

The third rung, and a surprise inside it

Rung 3 is where models look worst, and it is where we started. The verifier now handles it, but a confound in the translation step is still unresolved, so we have parked the main analysis. One result from that work stands on its own.

The largest rung 3 category, 1,422 questions, gives you a small world with rules like "the alarm rings if either person sets it" and asks what would have happened if one person had acted differently.

We wrote a 120 line program that does no reasoning whatsoever. It reads the rules, plugs in the values, and evaluates the logic.

Result
1,422 out of 1,422. A perfect score, from a program with no notion of causation in it at all.

So these questions do not test counterfactual reasoning. They test whether you can read a rule out of a paragraph. And that is exactly where models fail on them: the traces show the model guessing whether the rule is "either" or "both", when the paragraph says which in plain words.

Getting to a perfect score required one extra step that is worth reporting. The benchmark's own worked solutions sometimes describe a variable in terms of something that is not actually its cause in the diagram. A model that follows those descriptions literally will push a change down an arrow that does not exist. That affects 15.6% of the questions in this category.

Honesty

What we do not know

This is two models

Gemini 2.5 Flash and Claude Sonnet 4.6. They disagree on the direction of the main effect, which is a reason not to generalise. A third model would help, but most providers do not return usable working.

Two question types tell us almost nothing

Correlation and explaining away can both be answered from the phrasing of the question. Their high scores should not be read as evidence about the model. The conclusions rest on average effect, overall probability and collider bias.

The error breakdown is 66 cases

Enough to see that one bucket dominates. Not enough for precise proportions. And one bucket, the 21% for right value with wrong conclusion, is partly the coin flip problem rather than a real category.

The translation step is a second model

Everything about which quantity a model chose passes through Claude Sonnet reading prose. The controls show it reads the reasoning rather than guessing from the question, but it remains a single point of dependence. Running the same items through a different translator would test that, and we have not done it.

We changed the answer key

Six times, listed above with reasons. Four of those changes are corrections to the benchmark rather than to our code, but a reader who disagrees with any of them should recompute without it.

Rung 3 is open

The verifier handles it now. The translation confound there is still around 12 points, which is too large to draw conclusions through.

What this adds up to. On the first two rungs of CLadder, the accuracy score is not mainly a measure of causal reasoning. Roughly one question in six is graded wrong on formatting. Of the genuine errors, around nine in ten had the causal step right and failed at arithmetic afterwards. And a slice of the questions are decided by rounding rather than by reasoning at all.

None of that means models reason about causes well. It means this number was not the evidence.
